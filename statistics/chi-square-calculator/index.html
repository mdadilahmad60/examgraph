Chi-Square Calculator

About this calculator

Updated November 2026 · Free, runs in your browser

A chi-square test compares counts. The goodness-of-fit test asks whether observed counts match an expected distribution, such as whether a die is fair. The test of independence asks whether two categorical variables are related, using a table of counts. Enter your counts and the calculator returns the chi-square statistic, degrees of freedom, p-value, expected counts, each cell's contribution and a shaded graph of the p-value.

You may also find these tools useful: T-Test Calculator, Confidence Interval Calculator and Standard Deviation Calculator.

Disclaimer: ExamGrapher's statistics tools are for learning and for checking homework. Everything is calculated in your browser, and nothing you type is uploaded. For research, medical, financial or other high-stakes decisions, confirm results with dedicated statistical software.

Which chi-square test do I need?

  • Goodness of fit: one categorical variable, compared with an expected distribution. Degrees of freedom = categories − 1.
  • Test of independence: two categorical variables in a contingency table. Degrees of freedom = (rows − 1) × (columns − 1).

Formulas

  • Chi-square statistic: χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E, where O is the observed count and E is the expected count
  • Independence, expected count: E = (row total × column total) / grand total
  • Cramér’s V (effect size for a table): √(χ² / (n × min(rows − 1, columns − 1)))

Worked example: is a die fair?

A die is rolled 60 times and the six faces come up 5, 8, 9, 8, 10 and 20 times. If the die is fair, each face is expected 10 times. The contributions (O − E)² / E are 2.5, 0.4, 0.1, 0.4, 0 and 10, so χ² = 13.4 with 5 degrees of freedom and a p-value of about 0.0199. At α = 0.05 you reject the idea that the die is fair. The calculator opens with these counts.

Worked example: independence in a 2 × 2 table

Switch to the test of independence and use the table 30, 20 / 20, 30. Every expected count is 25, so χ² = 4 × (5² / 25) = 4 with 1 degree of freedom, giving p ≈ 0.0455.

Conditions to check

  • Use counts, not percentages or averages.
  • Observations are independent and come from a random sample.
  • Each expected count should be at least 5. The calculator warns you when it is not.

Practice problem: a goodness-of-fit test

A game spinner has five equal sections. In 100 spins the counts are 18, 22, 20, 25 and 15. If the spinner is fair each section is expected 20 times.

The statistic is (18-20)^2/20 + (22-20)^2/20 + 0 + (25-20)^2/20 + (15-20)^2/20 = (4 + 4 + 0 + 25 + 25)/20 = 2.9, with 5 - 1 = 4 degrees of freedom. The p-value is about 0.575, so these counts are consistent with a fair spinner.

Common mistakes

  • Entering percentages instead of counts. The test needs raw counts.
  • Using a chi-square test on repeated measures of the same people, which breaks independence.
  • Forgetting that a significant result says the categories are related, not why.

Frequently asked questions

What does the p-value tell me?

It is the probability, if the null hypothesis is true, of getting a chi-square statistic at least as large as yours. A p-value below your significance level is evidence against the null hypothesis. It does not tell you how strong or important the effect is.

Can the chi-square statistic be negative?

No. Every term (O − E)² / E is zero or positive, so the statistic is zero or positive. Large values mean the observed counts are far from the expected ones.

What if some expected counts are below 5?

The chi-square approximation becomes less reliable. Combine categories where that makes sense, collect more data, or use an exact test such as Fisher's exact test for a 2 × 2 table.

Does it use the Yates continuity correction?

No. It uses the standard formula taught in most courses. Some software applies a continuity correction to 2 × 2 tables by default, which gives a slightly different p-value.

Can I enter percentages?

For the independence test, enter counts. For goodness of fit, enter observed counts, and you can give the expected distribution as proportions or percentages in the custom proportions option.

What does Cramér's V mean?

It measures how strongly two variables are associated in a table, from 0 (no association) to 1 (perfect association). It helps because a large sample can make a tiny effect statistically significant.
