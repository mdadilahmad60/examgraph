T-Test Calculator

About this calculator

Updated November 2026 · Free, runs in your browser

A t-test checks whether a sample mean differs from a value, or whether two means differ, when the population standard deviation is unknown. Choose the test type, enter raw data or summary statistics, and the calculator returns the t statistic, degrees of freedom, p-value, a confidence interval and an effect size, with a shaded graph of the p-value.

Good next steps from here: Confidence Interval Calculator, Chi-Square Calculator and Standard Deviation Calculator.

Disclaimer: ExamGrapher's statistics tools are for learning and for checking homework. Everything is calculated in your browser, and nothing you type is uploaded. For research, medical, financial or other high-stakes decisions, confirm results with dedicated statistical software.

Which t-test should I use?

  • One-sample: compare one sample mean to a known or claimed value.
  • Two-sample (Welch): compare the means of two independent groups without assuming equal variances. This is the safer default.
  • Two-sample (pooled): the same comparison, but it assumes both groups have the same variance, and uses n₁ + n₂ − 2 degrees of freedom.
  • Paired: compare two measurements on the same subjects, such as before and after. The test runs on the differences.

Formulas

  • One-sample: t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s/√n), with n − 1 degrees of freedom
  • Welch: t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / √(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂), with Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom
  • Paired: t = d̄ / (sᵈ/√n), where d is each pair's difference

Worked example

Sample 1 is 23, 25, 28, 30, 26, 27, 24, 29 (mean 26.5) and sample 2 is 20, 22, 25, 24, 21, 23, 26, 22 (mean 22.875). A Welch two-sample test gives t ≈ 3.22 with about 13.5 degrees of freedom and a two-sided p-value of about 0.0064. At α = 0.05 that is enough to reject the null hypothesis that the means are equal. The calculator opens with this data.

Reading the p-value

The p-value is the probability, assuming the null hypothesis is true, of getting a t statistic at least as extreme as yours. A p-value below your significance level α is evidence against the null hypothesis. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and a non-significant result does not prove the means are equal.

Assumptions

  • Observations are independent (or properly paired).
  • The data is roughly normal, or the samples are reasonably large.
  • There are no extreme outliers. Plot your data if you are unsure.

Practice problem: a paired test

Six students take a quiz before and after a review session. Before: 72, 75, 68, 80, 77, 71. After: 78, 79, 70, 85, 80, 75. The differences are 6, 4, 2, 5, 3, 4, with mean 4 and standard deviation about 1.414.

The standard error is 1.414/sqrt(6), about 0.577, so t = 4/0.577, about 6.93 with 5 degrees of freedom. The two-sided p-value is about 0.00096, strong evidence that scores changed. Enter the two lists in the paired test to see the same result.

Common mistakes

  • Using a two-sample test on paired data. If the same people are measured twice, use the paired test.
  • Choosing a one-tailed test after seeing which way the data points. Decide the direction before collecting data.
  • Reading a large p-value as proof of no difference. It only means the data did not give enough evidence.
  • Ignoring a very small sample with strong skew or outliers, where the t-test can mislead.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use Welch or the pooled t-test?

Welch is the safer choice because it does not assume equal variances and loses little power when the variances are equal. Use the pooled test when your course or problem tells you to assume equal variances.

When do I use a one-tailed test?

Only when your hypothesis names a direction before you look at the data, such as “the new method scores higher”. Otherwise use the two-sided test.

What are degrees of freedom?

They describe how much independent information the standard deviation estimate rests on. For a one-sample or paired test it is n − 1. The Welch test uses a fractional value.

What does Cohen's d mean?

It is an effect size: the difference between means in standard deviation units. As a rough guide, 0.2 is small, 0.5 is medium and 0.8 is large, but what counts as large depends on the field.

Can I use a t-test on a small sample?

Yes, if the data is roughly normal. With very small samples the test is sensitive to outliers and skew.

Why does the paired test need two lists of the same length?

Each value in the first list is matched with the value in the same position in the second list, and the test runs on those differences.
